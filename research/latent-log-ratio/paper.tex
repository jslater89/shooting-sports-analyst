	\begin{abstract}
		Traditional rating systems like Elo, Glicko, and TrueSkill were designed for zero-sum or ordinal competitions. Applying them to continuous, proportional-scoring sports (e.g., practical shooting, racing) introduces severe structural friction. Existing systems either discard continuous margins of victory entirely, or fall victim to the ``winner's curse''—a mathematical artifact where an outlier winning performance artificially deflates the apparent skill of the entire field.

		This paper proposes the Latent Log-Ratio Model, a Bayesian state-space approach that operates natively on continuous, ratio-to-winner scoring. By leveraging the logarithmic structure of multiplicative performances, the model cleanly isolates pairwise skill gaps and exactly cancels the winner's nuisance variance.

		The system features $\mathcal{O}(N)$ complexity in its simplest form, explicitly decoupled tracking of epistemic uncertainty and aleatoric performance dispersion, and an optional selection of statistical tools to handle degenerate fields without artifacting. Finally, it retains trivial reversibility from ratings to performance ratios, enabling the generation of predictions over complete fields of competitors.
	\end{abstract}

	\section{Key Insights and Concepts}

	\subsection{The Multiplicative Nature of Scoring}
	In practical shooting, true skill is fundamentally multiplicative, whether measured by hit factor  in USPSA, or total time in time-plus sports like IDPA and ICORE. If Competitor A takes 10 seconds to shoot a stage and Competitor B takes 20 seconds, Competitor A is twice as fast. On a longer stage, the times might be 20 seconds and 40 seconds; the absolute margin changes, but the $2\times$ ratio remains constant.

	\subsection{The ``Winner's Curse'' in Percentage Scoring}
	Matches are scored as percentages of the top competitor's performance. Using these raw percentages injects the winner's match-day variance into the entire field, a phenomenon we term the ``winner's curse'' (distinct from the overbidding phenomenon in auction theory).
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Hit Factor} (USPSA): Each competitor's stage score is their points divided by their time (the ``hit factor''). The stage percentage is this hit factor divided by the winner's hit factor. On each stage, competitors receive a fraction of the total points available from that stage determined by their finish ratio in \([0, 1]\). A match score is the sum of all stage scores.
		\item \textbf{Time-Plus} (IDPA/ICORE): Each competitor's stage score is their raw time plus penalties. The implied percentage is the winner's time divided by the competitor's time.
	\end{itemize}
	If the match winner has an exceptionally good day, every other competitor's percentage drops, mathematically suggesting the field performed poorly when they did not.

	\subsection{The Logarithmic Bridge}
	Let $P_i$ denote competitor $i$'s \textit{latent} (true) performance parameter on any positive ratio scale where larger is better. On match day, each competitor produces a realized performance $X_i$---a noisy draw reflecting their current skill. The scoreboard reports only the ratio of realized performances:
	\begin{equation}
		S_i = \frac{X_i}{\xwin}
	\end{equation}
	where $\xwin = \max_j X_j$ is the realized performance of whoever won the match. Critically, neither $P_i$ nor $X_i$ is directly observed; only the ratio $S_i$ is published.

	For any two competitors $A$ and $B$, the difference of their log-percentages is
	\begin{equation}
		\ln(S_A) - \ln(S_B) = \ln\!\left(\frac{X_A}{\xwin}\right) - \ln\!\left(\frac{X_B}{\xwin}\right)
	\end{equation}
	Using $\ln(x/y) = \ln(x) - \ln(y)$, this becomes
	\begin{equation}
		\ln(S_A) - \ln(S_B) = (\ln(X_A) - \ln(\xwin)) - (\ln(X_B) - \ln(\xwin))
	\end{equation}
	and the common anchor cancels exactly:
	\begin{equation}
		\ln(S_A) - \ln(S_B) = \ln(X_A) - \ln(X_B) = \ln\!\left(\frac{X_A}{X_B}\right)
	\end{equation}
	This is the central trick of the model: the realized winning performance $\xwin$ appears only as a nuisance normalization constant in the published percentages; it does not survive pairwise comparison in log space, an idea derived from compositional data analysis\cite{aitchison1982compositional}\footnote{Although this model leverages log-ratio transformations that are fundamental to compositional data analysis, match percentages do not themselves constitute compositional data by the strict definition, as they violate the unit-sum constraint. The data do, however, have scale invariance: the absolute magnitudes are irrelevant or (in our case) misleading, and all the usable information is contained strictly in the \emph{ratios} between the components.}. The resulting quantity is the pure ratio of the competitors' realized performances, independent of who won the match or by how much.

	Because each realized performance is a noisy draw from around the competitor's latent skill, the log-ratio decomposes as:
	\begin{equation}
		\ln\!\left(\frac{X_A}{X_B}\right) = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_A}{P_B}\right) + (\varepsilon_A - \varepsilon_B)
	\end{equation}
	where $\varepsilon_i$ represents competitor $i$'s match-day noise. In expectation, the noise cancels and the observed log-ratio is a direct estimate of the latent skill gap:
	\begin{equation}
		E\!\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{X_A}{X_B}\right)\right] = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_A}{P_B}\right) \approx R_A - R_B
	\end{equation}
	In time-plus sports, this same derivation applies by defining $X_i$ as any proportional speed variable such as $\tmin/T_i$, which yields $\ln(S_A) - \ln(S_B) = \ln(T_B/T_A)$.

	\subsection{Ratings as Expected Log-Ratios}
	This cancellation property leads directly to the model's core identity. Since ratings $R_i$ track each competitor's current latent log-performance $\ln(P_i)$, the difference between any two ratings is the expected log-ratio of their realized performances:
	\begin{equation}
		R_i - R_j = E[\ln(S_i) - \ln(S_j)] = E\!\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{X_i}{X_j}\right)\right] = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_i}{P_j}\right)
	\end{equation}

	Equivalently, the median ratio of two competitors' realized performances is:
	\begin{equation}
		\median\!\left(\frac{X_i}{X_j}\right) = \exp(R_i - R_j)
	\end{equation}

	The rating difference \textit{is} the expected performance difference: a rating gap of $\ln(2) \approx 0.69$ means one competitor's latent skill is twice the level of the other. Deep-tail pathologies are handled by inflating observation noise for very weak finishes, not by deforming the log-ratio mean link, so the prediction mapping remains globally reversible through the exponential function.

	\subsection{The Minimal Filter}
	\label{sec:minimal}
	The identity above is not yet a rating system. It says what $R_i - R_j$ \emph{means}; it does not say how to move $R_i$ when a new scoreboard arrives. The smallest filter that respects the identity is an ordinary Kalman update on reconstructed absolute log-performance. Each competitor is a Gaussian $(R_i, V_i)$---a point estimate of $\ln(P_i)$ and the epistemic uncertainty around it---and the only sport-level noise is a scalar $\ssport$.

	Assume a generative draw with i.i.d.\ match-day noise and independent rating priors:
	\begin{equation}
		\ln(X_i) = \ln(P_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \ssport).
	\end{equation}
	The scoreboard still publishes only $S_i = X_i / \xwin$. Translating to log space, $L_i = \ln(S_i) = \ln(X_i) - \ln(\xwin)$, yields a vector of relative measurements whose common anchor is the unobserved winning performance. Kalman-filtering $L_i$ directly would treat that anchor as if it were the competitor's skill, reintroducing the winner's curse. The filter's first job is therefore to estimate $\ln(\xwin)$ from the field and add it back.

	Write $V_i$ for the current prior variance, including ordinary process-noise aging $V_i + \sdrift \cdot \Delta t$ when $\Delta t$ rating periods have elapsed since the last commit. Weight each competitor by the precision of that prior against sport noise,
	\begin{equation}
		W_i = \frac{1}{V_i + \ssport},
	\end{equation}
	and form the residual $a_i = R_i - L_i$. Because $E[R_i - \ln(X_i)] = 0$, each $a_i$ is a noisy observation of $\ln(\xwin)$: the field collectively answers, ``given what we already believe about these competitors, what realized performance corresponded to 100\% today?'' A leave-one-out precision-weighted consensus keeps competitor $i$ from anchoring themselves:
	\begin{equation}
		\bloo = \frac{\sum_{j \neq i} W_j a_j}{\sum_{j \neq i} W_j}, \qquad
		\mathrm{Var}(\bloo) = \frac{1}{\sum_{j \neq i} W_j}.
	\end{equation}
	The variance formula is the independent-prior case: a large field of well-known competitors produces a sharp anchor; a large field of unknowns does not, because every $W_j$ is small and $\mathrm{Var}(\bloo)$ refuses to collapse. Adding the anchor cancels the nuisance exactly,
	\begin{equation}
		O_i = L_i + \bloo \approx \ln(X_i),
	\end{equation}
	and the associated observation noise compounds sport noise with the uncertainty of the reconstruction:
	\begin{equation}
		R_{\mathrm{obs},i} = \ssport + \mathrm{Var}(\bloo).
	\end{equation}

	The rest is the textbook scalar Kalman step. The innovation, total uncertainty, and gain are
	\begin{equation}
		e_i = O_i - R_i, \qquad
		T_i = V_i + R_{\mathrm{obs},i}, \qquad
		K_i = \frac{V_i}{T_i},
	\end{equation}
	and the posterior is
	\begin{equation}
		R_i \leftarrow R_i + K_i e_i, \qquad
		V_i \leftarrow V_i (1 - K_i).
	\end{equation}

	This is already a complete, $\mathcal{O}(N)$, globally reversible rating system. A gap of $\ln(2)$ still means a twofold skill ratio; $\exp(R_i - R_j)$ is still the median performance ratio. What it cannot do is distinguish ``this person is noisy'' from ``we do not yet know this person,'' distinguish a breakout from a catastrophic equipment failure, or refuse to trust a geometrically degenerate scoreboard. The following statistical machinery attempts to address those faults.

	\section{System Architecture and Algorithm}

	\subsection{Generative Model}
	The production filter uses the same latent-log generative model as Section~\ref{sec:minimal}, with one addition: match-day noise includes a per-competitor aleatoric term.
	\begin{equation}
		\ln(X_i) = \ln(P_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \ssport + \sigmai)
	\end{equation}
	The noise $\varepsilon_i$ now combines the irreducible environmental noise of the sport ($\ssport$) with competitor $i$'s individual performance dispersion ($\sigmai$). Ratings $R_i$ and variances $V_i$ retain their meaning from the skeleton: a Gaussian posterior over $\ln(P_i)$.

	\subsection{State Representation}
	The skeleton's two-parameter state $(R_i, V_i)$ is retained. The production filter adds two auxiliary trackers:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$R_i$, $V_i$:} The Gaussian posterior of Section~\ref{sec:minimal}. An average baseline competitor can be anchored at $R_i = 0$.
		\item \textbf{$\sigmai$ (Performance Dispersion):} The aleatoric uncertainty in a rating: ``how much does a competitor's performance naturally vary from event to event?'' Tracks whether a competitor is consistent (i.e., sees generally small rating updates) or prone to large swings (sees large ones).
		\item \textbf{$m_i$ (Innovation Momentum):} An exponential moving average of recent prediction errors. Used to distinguish directional skill trends (breakouts or collapses) from zero-mean aleatoric scatter.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{The Core Match Processing and Update Loop}
	This section elaborates the skeleton of Section~\ref{sec:minimal} into the production core, using the primary parameters. It assumes normally distributed log-ratios, strict ratio scales, and statistically well-behaved fields. The computational complexity is $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot k)$ where $k$ is the number of pairwise opponents evaluated per competitor.

	\subsubsection*{Algorithm Overview}
	The Latent Log-Ratio update processes each match through a sequence of mathematical phases that separate the structural topology of the match from individual performance observations. The procedure consists of the following steps:

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Translation:} $L_i = \ln(S_i)$, as in Section~\ref{sec:minimal}.

		\item \textbf{Anchoring (Baseline Estimation):} The leave-one-out baseline $\bloo$ of Section~\ref{sec:minimal} is retained, with two additions: Huber robust weights, and off-diagonal cohort covariance $\rhoc$ so that unknown fields remain a floating anchor rather than a rigid datum.

		\item \textbf{Observation Variance Construction:} The total observation variance \(R_{\mathrm{obs},i}\) is formed as the sum of generative physical noise (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2\), certainty-scaled individual dispersion \(\tilde{\sigma}_i^2\)), baseline estimation variance \(\mathrm{Var}(B_{\mathrm{obs}})\), and reliability/misspecification penalties (topological novelty \(\sigma_{\mathrm{novel}}^2\) in the core loop; deep-tail noise \(\eta(S_i)\) and weak-field pathology \(\sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2\) in the extended model). An observation quality factor \(Q_i\) isolates generative physical components from those reliability penalties.

		\item \textbf{Robust Mean Update:} The scalar Kalman step of Section~\ref{sec:minimal}, with two changes: the prior $V_i^{\mathrm{prior}}$ is Trend-inflated (next step), and a Student-$t$ weight $w_t(z_i)$ is applied exclusively to the mean update.

		\item \textbf{State-Space Adaptation:}
		The innovation \(e_i = O_i - R_i\) is processed into a quality-gated version \(\ephys\), stripping reliability/misspecification penalties while retaining generative physical outliers under a trustworthy log-link, and a robust version \(\erobust\), which additionally applies a Student-$t$ robust weighting to limit the influence of extreme single-event anomalies, through the observation quality factor \(Q_i\).
		Two complementary mechanisms adapt the epistemic uncertainty. A \textit{Trend} term, derived from the committed momentum \(m_i\) carried forward from prior matches, acts as adaptive process noise and is injected into the prior \emph{before} the Kalman update: \(V_i^{\mathrm{prior}} = V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + \gamma \cdot \text{Trend}_i\). This raises the Kalman gain when the filter has accumulated evidence of sustained skill drift, allowing the mean update to respond. A \textit{Shock} term, derived from the current match's quality-gated innovation, is injected into the posterior independently: \(V_i \leftarrow V_i^{\mathrm{prior}}(1 - K_i) + \gamma \cdot \text{Shock}_i\). Shock widens uncertainty after a single surprising observation without forcing the mean to chase it, and earns its way into a subsequent mean response only if the inflated posterior survives into the next match's prior.
		Meanwhile, the aleatoric dispersion \(\sigmai\) and momentum \(m_i\) receive exponential-moving-average updates.

	\end{enumerate}

	\subsubsection*{Step 1: Calculate Log-Scores}
	As in Section~\ref{sec:minimal}, $L_i = \ln(S_i)$.
	\textit{Note: The core loop assumes strictly positive $S_i$. Safe boundaries and tail non-linearities are handled in the extended model (Section 2.4).}

	\subsubsection*{Step 2: Age the Prior and Calculate Core Competitor Weights}
	Age on entry as in Section~\ref{sec:minimal}, now with an explicit ceiling:
	\begin{equation}
		V_i^{\mathrm{age}} = \min\!\left(V_{\max},\; V_i + \sdrift \times \Delta t\right)
	\end{equation}
	The posterior written at the end of the update is this match's Kalman $V_i$ (Step 8); it is not aged again until the next event.

	Production weights also include the competitor's aleatoric dispersion:
	\begin{equation}
		W_i = \frac{1}{V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + \ssport + \sigmai}
	\end{equation}
	High-variance and high-dispersion competitors naturally receive less influence.

	\subsubsection*{Step 3: Establish the Core Match Baseline ($B_{-i}$)}
	The residual $a_i = R_i - L_i$ and leave-one-out consensus $\bloo$ are those of Section~\ref{sec:minimal}: $a_i$ is a noisy observation of $\ln(\xwin)$, and $\bloo$ is the field's estimate of what 100\% corresponded to today.

	To prevent extreme outliers (e.g., a competitor suffering a catastrophic equipment failure) from dragging the global mathematical anchor, we apply a Huber-style robust weighting. First, compute a raw, prior-regularized baseline using the core weights:
	\begin{equation}
		B_{\mathrm{raw}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N W_i a_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N W_i}
	\end{equation}
	Standardize each residual relative to this raw center and compute a tapered robust weight using the baseline robustness threshold $c$:
	\begin{equation}
		r_i = \frac{a_i - B_{\mathrm{raw}}}{\sqrt{1/W_i}}, \qquad \tilde{W}_i = W_i \cdot \min\!\left(1, \frac{c}{|r_i|}\right)
	\end{equation}

	We then form a precision-weighted leave-one-out consensus estimate of the winner's performance for each competitor using these robust weights:
	\begin{equation}
		B_{-i} = \frac{\sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{W}_j a_j}{\sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{W}_j}
	\end{equation}

	To formalize the missing off-diagonal covariance in an $\mathcal{O}(N)$ framework, we introduce $\rho$, the intraclass correlation of priors. Assuming a uniform prior correlation $\rho$ among the cohort's epistemic uncertainties, the exact analytical variance of the precision-weighted baseline expands to include the sum of all pairwise covariances. By factoring the cross-terms, this reduces algebraically to an $\mathcal{O}(N)$ difference of sums using the robust weights:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathrm{Var}(B_{-i}) = \frac{1}{\sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{W}_j} + \frac{\rho}{\left(\sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{W}_j\right)^2} \left[ \left( \sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{W}_j \sqrt{V_j^{\mathrm{age}}} \right)^2 - \sum_{j \neq i} \tilde{W}_j^2 V_j^{\mathrm{age}} \right]
	\end{equation}
	This exact formulation guarantees that a large field of unknown competitors provides a \emph{floating} anchor rather than a rigid one, correctly suppressing the Kalman gain for the winner and preserving their epistemic uncertainty.

	\subsubsection*{Step 4: Calculate Local Baseline ($B_{\mathrm{local}}$)}
	The match baseline captures average field difficulty but discards structural information about \textit{whom} each competitor outperformed. For each competitor $i$, we select a subset of opponents $\mathcal{S}_i$ (e.g., competitors shooting the same schedule, or competitors close in rating or finish). For each opponent $j$, compute the log-space pairwise residual:
	\begin{equation}
		\delta_{ij} = (L_i - L_j) - (R_i - R_j)
	\end{equation}
	Crucially, because $L_i - L_j = \ln(X_i/\xwin) - \ln(X_j/\xwin) = \ln(X_i/X_j)$, the $\xwin$ anchor cancels out entirely. This pairwise comparison isolates the pure ratio of realized performances and is structurally immune to the winner's curse.

	Through algebraic rearrangement, we can express this residual using the terms from Step 3:
	\begin{equation}
		\delta_{ij} = (L_i - R_i) + (R_j - L_j) = (L_i - R_i) + a_j
	\end{equation}
	The appearance of $a_j$ allows us to reframe our pairwise comparison as a local baseline $B_{\mathrm{local}}$ in the exact same manner as the global match baseline. To ensure the local baseline properly inherits both the Huber robustification and the cohort uncertainty of the subset, we reuse the Step~3 weights $\tilde{W}_j$ and the same hierarchical variance formulation:
	\begin{equation}
		B_{\mathrm{local}} = \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_i} \tilde{W}_j a_j}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_i} \tilde{W}_j}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
		\mathrm{Var}(B_{\mathrm{local}}) = \frac{1}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_i} \tilde{W}_j} + \frac{\rho}{\left(\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_i} \tilde{W}_j\right)^2} \left[ \left( \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_i} \tilde{W}_j \sqrt{V_j^{\mathrm{age}}} \right)^2 - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_i} \tilde{W}_j^2 V_j^{\mathrm{age}} \right]
	\end{equation}

	\subsubsection*{Step 5: Calculate Observed Performance ($O_i$)}
	Let the aggregate observed baseline be $B_{\mathrm{obs}} = (1 - \alpha_i) \cdot B_{-i} + \alpha_i \cdot B_{\mathrm{local}}$. The competitor's absolute observed performance is simply their log-score shifted by this baseline:
	\begin{equation}
		O_i = L_i + B_{\mathrm{obs}}
	\end{equation}
	To understand why this recovers the realized performance, consider the pure baseline case\footnote{\(\blocal\) is simply an alternative estimate of the exact same underlying quantity (the winner's realized performance $\xwin$). It is omitted here strictly for notational simplicity; its inclusion does not alter the fundamental cancellation.} ($\alpha = 0$, so $B_{\mathrm{obs}} = \bloo$). Because the baseline is the field's estimate of $\ln(\xwin)$, substituting $L_i$ reveals:
	\begin{align*}
		O_i &\approx (\ln(X_i) - \ln(\xwin)) + \ln(\xwin) \\
		&= \ln(X_i)
	\end{align*}
	By adding the baseline to the log-score, the nuisance anchor perfectly cancels out. The observed performance $O_i$ therefore recovers $\ln(X_i)$---the competitor's realized match-day log-performance on the absolute global rating scale.

	When the local opponent set $\mathcal{S}_i$ approaches the full field, $B_{\mathrm{local}}$ duplicates $B_{-i}$ and the blend contributes no additional structural information; $\alpha_i$ may be tapered toward 0 in that limit so the update remains a clean global baseline.

	Empirical testing on well-mixed, large-field matches demonstrates that $B_{\mathrm{local}}$ frequently converges with $B_{-i}$, allowing $\alpha$ to be tuned to 0 for a strictly $\mathcal{O}(N)$ global-only update. However, retaining the pairwise blend enables the model to gracefully handle stratified fields. In practical shooting, competitors often shoot in scheduled squads across multiple days, exposing them to varying environmental conditions (e.g., a ``super squad'' of elite competitors shooting in heavy rain, while the rest of the field shoots a different schedule in ideal weather). By blending in $B_{\mathrm{local}}$, the model leans on local peer comparisons---competitors facing the exact same physical and environmental topology---preventing the global baseline from washing out localized structural truths.

	\subsubsection*{Step 6: Total Observation Variance and Topological Novelty}
	\label{step6}
	To determine how much weight to assign the observation, we must establish its total variance. First, to prevent a highly uncertain competitor from being artificially shielded from updates by their own behavioral dispersion, we strictly subordinate aleatoric throttling to epistemic certainty. We define a certainty factor $c_i \in [0,1]$ and an effective discounted dispersion $\tilde{\sigma}_i^2$:
	\begin{equation}
		c_i = 1 - \min\!\left(1,\frac{V_i^{\mathrm{age}}}{V_{\max}}\right), \qquad \tilde{\sigma}_i^2 = \sigma_i^2 \cdot c_i
	\end{equation}
	For an established veteran, $c_i \approx 1.0$ and their full behavioral dispersion is respected. For a new or highly uncertain competitor, $c_i$ approaches $0$, ensuring the filter remains fluid.

	Second, because the observed baseline $B_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is a convex blend of two estimates, we must explicitly propagate its structural estimation variance. Although $B_{-i}$ and $B_{\mathrm{local}}$ are not independent, the subset structure implies that their covariance is exactly $\mathrm{Var}(B_{-i})$ under independent priors ($\rho = 0$). With a nonzero intraclass correlation $\rho$, this equality becomes an approximation, as the precision-weighted average of the square roots of the opponent variances ($\sqrt{V_i}$) in the local subset $\mathcal{S}_i$ will generally differ slightly from that of the full field. Nevertheless, the variance of the blended baseline simplifies elegantly to:

	\begin{align}
		\mathrm{Var}(\bobs) &= (1 - \alpha_i)^2 \mathrm{Var}(B_{-i}) + \alpha_i^2 \mathrm{Var}(B_{\mathrm{local}}) + 2\alpha_i(1 - \alpha_i)\mathrm{Var}(B_{-i}) \nonumber\\
		&= (1 - \alpha_i^2) \mathrm{Var}(B_{-i}) + \alpha_i^2 \mathrm{Var}(B_{\mathrm{local}})
	\end{align}

	We define the sum of fundamental sport noise, effective dispersion, and this structural estimation variance as the \textit{clean} observation variance:
	\begin{equation}
		R_{\mathrm{clean},i} = \sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2 + \tilde{\sigma}_i^2 + \mathrm{Var}(B_{\mathrm{obs}})
	\end{equation}

	Finally, we must apply the second half of the off-diagonal covariance approximation. While the intraclass correlation $\rho$ successfully prevents the baseline variance of unrated fields from collapsing to zero, the Kalman filter's epistemic variance $V_i$ still decays proportionally to $1/N$. Therefore, \textit{statistical certainty} within a local cohort outpaces its \textit{topological mixing} with the global graph. A new club consisting of unrated shooters might rapidly establish accurate relative rankings among themselves, but it requires an accumulation of events over time for those shooters to solidly anchor their local sub-graph to the global mean.

	To formalize this macro-level structural uncertainty, we treat graph isolation as a form of environmental penalty noise. Event history proxies the time required for network mixing. Let $k_i$ be the historical experience of competitor $i$. Using the graph maturity threshold $k_{\mathrm{max}}$, the logarithmic maturity fraction for each competitor is:
	\begin{equation}
		\mu_i = \min\!\left(1.0,\; \frac{\ln(k_i + 1)}{\ln(k_{\mathrm{max}} + 1)}\right)
	\end{equation}
	Field maturity is the $W$-weighted mean of those fractions, using the core competitor weights of Step~2:
	\begin{equation}
		\bar{\mu} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^N W_j \mu_j}{\sum_{j=1}^N W_j}
	\end{equation}
	The weight $W_j$ is competitor $j$'s fractional contribution to the typical match anchor, not an inverse-variance on $\mu_j$ itself---$k_j$ is observed exactly. Huber weights $\tilde{W}_j$ are not reused here: a single-event residual outlier does not make a competitor less graph-mature. The active Field Novelty Penalty scales inversely with this precision-mass maturity, bounded by the maximum novelty variance $\psi^2$:
	\begin{equation}
		\sigma_{\mathrm{novel}}^2 = \psi^2 \cdot (1 - \bar{\mu})
	\end{equation}
	When $\bar{\mu} = 0$ (a field whose precision mass is entirely new), the penalty reaches $\psi^2$, safely throttling the update velocity for the entire cohort. A connected veteran in an otherwise new field keeps $\bar{\mu}$ high, so newcomers are not treated as an isolated island when a global node is actually setting $B$. As the precision mass accumulates history, $\bar{\mu}$ rises logarithmically until the novelty penalty reaches exactly zero for mature fields.

	We partition observation variance into \emph{generative physical} noise---quantities that belong in the match-day likelihood under the log-ratio model---and \emph{reliability / misspecification} penalties that express skepticism about whether the observation's magnitude is a precise instrument for latent skill. In the core loop the only reliability term is novelty; the extended model (Section~2.4) adds deep-tail noise $\eta(S_i)$ and weak-field pathology $\sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2$. The total observation variance and Observation Quality scalar $Q_i \in (0,1]$ are therefore:
	\begin{equation}
		R_{\mathrm{obs},i} = R_{\mathrm{clean},i} + \sigma_{\mathrm{novel}}^2 + \eta(S_i) + \sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
		Q_i = \frac{R_{\mathrm{clean},i}}{R_{\mathrm{obs},i}}
	\end{equation}
	with $\eta(S_i) = \sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2 = 0$ in the idealized core. Because every reliability term sits outside $R_{\mathrm{clean},i}$, $Q_i$ automatically strips novelty, deep-tail skepticism, and weak-field pathology together.

	For the Kalman mean update (Step 7), observation quality is handled automatically: when reliability penalties inflate $R_{\mathrm{obs},i}$, the Kalman gain $K_i = V_i^{\mathrm{prior}} / (V_i^{\mathrm{prior}} + R_{\mathrm{obs},i})$ shrinks, and the rating barely moves. However, the momentum and dispersion trackers \mbox{(Steps 8--9)} are plain exponential moving averages with no built-in gain denominator---they process whatever innovation is fed to them at their configured adaptation rate. Without correction, a degenerate field inflated by novelty variance (or a geometrically unreliable deep-tail residual) would pump untrustworthy signal into these trackers at full strength.

	$Q_i$ fills this gap by playing the role of an observation-quality gate for the non-Kalman-filtered state variables. It is more selective than the Kalman gain: the gain discounts based on \emph{all} observation noise (generative and reliability alike), while $Q_i$ strips only the reliability / misspecification component, deliberately leaving generative physical noise intact. This selectivity is essential because the adaptation trackers measure different things about a competitor's behavior: large residuals under a trustworthy log-link \emph{should} reach the shock detector and shatter epistemic confidence, but artificial noise from unanchored fields---or score-dependent contamination whose reported magnitude is not a calibrated physical innovation---should not inflate momentum or dispersion.

	\subsubsection*{Step 7: Trend-Driven Prior Inflation and Mean Update}
	Before applying the Kalman mean update, we treat the competitor's carried-forward innovation momentum as evidence of adaptive process noise. Using the certainty factor $c_i$ from Step 6, define the effective momentum adaptation rate (reused in Step 8 for the momentum EMA itself):
	\begin{equation}
		\lambdad_i^{\mathrm{eff}} = \lambdad \cdot \max(0.25, c_i)
	\end{equation}
	The Trend term is computed from the \emph{committed} momentum $m_i$ (i.e., the value produced by prior matches, before this match's update):
	\begin{equation}
		\text{Trend}_i = \frac{m_i^2}{\lambdad_i^{\mathrm{eff}}}
	\end{equation}
	Trend acts as a one-match-delayed adaptive estimate of unmodelled skill drift beyond the fixed $\sdrift$ aging term. We inject it into the prior variance:
	\begin{equation}
		V_i^{\mathrm{prior}} = \min\!\left(V_{\max},\; V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + \gammad \cdot \text{Trend}_i\right)
	\end{equation}

	This is the canonical Kalman placement for process noise: time-step process noise belongs on the prior side of the update, and Trend is precisely the filter's own estimate of how much process noise the fixed $\sdrift$ term under-specified. Inflating the prior widens both the Kalman gain (allowing the mean to move further toward the observation when drift evidence has accumulated) and the posterior residual, maintaining self-consistency between the mean and variance responses.

	The raw innovation measures how surprising the competitor's observed performance was relative to their current expected rating:
	\begin{equation}
		e_i = O_i - R_i
	\end{equation}
	Define the total system variance against which the innovation is judged---now using the inflated prior---and construct the Kalman gain:
	\begin{equation}
		T_i = V_i^{\mathrm{prior}} + R_{\mathrm{obs},i}, \qquad K_i = \frac{V_i^{\mathrm{prior}}}{T_i}
	\end{equation}

	Even with correctly scaled observation noise, individual match results can be corrupted by catastrophic physical anomalies (e.g., equipment failure or severe procedural penalties). To protect the core rating estimate, we introduce a heavy-tailed Student-$t$ downweighting factor for extreme innovations. Calculate a normalized $z$-score against the total system uncertainty $T_i$:
	\begin{equation}
		z_i = \frac{e_i}{\sqrt{T_i}}
	\end{equation}
	Define a robust scalar using a degrees-of-freedom parameter \(\nudof > 0\) (default \(4\)) and a full-strength innovation cutoff \(\ctcut \ge 0\) (in innovation sigmas):
	\begin{equation}
		w_t(z_i) = \min\!\left(1,\frac{\nudof + \ctcut^2}{\nudof + z_i^2}\right)
	\end{equation}
	Setting \(\ctcut = 1\) recovers the standard Student-$t$ kernel; larger values admit a wider innovation band at full strength before tapering begins. The cutoff may optionally be split by the sign of \(e_i\) (a larger \(\ctcut\) on positive innovations, a smaller one on negative) when the sport's contamination is known to be one-sided; the kernel itself is unchanged.
	The rating moves toward the observation using the robust Kalman mean update:
	\begin{equation}
		R_i \leftarrow R_i + K_i \cdot w_t(z_i) \cdot e_i
	\end{equation}
	Crucially, this downweighting applies \emph{only} to the mean rating update. The variance surprise logic and performance dispersion trackers must process physical anomalies as legitimate shocks to epistemic certainty.

	\subsubsection*{Step 8: Update Momentum and Committed Variance (Shock Adaptation)}
	To properly handle the bifurcated needs of momentum (which tracks sustained, statistically valid trends) and single-event shocks (which must react to generative physical deviations without being artificially inflated by reliability penalties), we define two distinct scaled innovations using the Observation Quality scalar $Q_i$:
	\begin{align}
		\ephys &= e_i \cdot Q_i \\
		\erobust &= e_i \cdot w_t(z_i) \cdot Q_i
	\end{align}
	The \textit{physical innovation} $\ephys$ strips reliability / misspecification penalties (novelty, deep-tail noise, and weak-field pathology) but retains heavy-tailed generative outliers under a trustworthy log-link. The \textit{robust innovation} $\erobust$ additionally applies the Student-$t$ damping to ignore single-event anomalies.

	Update the competitor's innovation momentum using the robust innovation, ensuring it only builds upon believable, sustained trends. The certainty-scaled effective adaptation rate $\lambda_i^{\mathrm{eff}}$ was defined in Step 7 and is reused here:
	\begin{equation}
		m_i \leftarrow (1 - \lambda_i^{\mathrm{eff}}) m_i + \lambda_i^{\mathrm{eff}} \erobust
	\end{equation}
	The updated $m_i$ is committed to the competitor's state and drives the Trend injection on the \emph{next} match's prior (Step 7, next iteration).

	Evaluate the system for a single-event shock using the physical innovation against the inflated total system variance. This guarantees that massive outliers caused by degenerate fields, unanchored cohorts, or geometrically unreliable deep-tail finishes are safely ignored, but massive outliers caused by generative physical deviations under a trustworthy log-link legitimately shatter the filter's epistemic confidence:
	\begin{equation}
		\text{Shock}_i = \max\!\left(0,\; (\ephys)^2 - T_i\right)
	\end{equation}
	Because $T_i$ already absorbs the Trend-authorized process noise from Step 7, Shock fires only on surprises that exceed even the trend-inflated expectation, which avoids double-counting when Trend and an outlier event co-occur.

	Shock is injected directly into the posterior variance, without passing through the Kalman contraction factor:
	\begin{equation}
		V_i \leftarrow \min\!\left(V_{\max},\; V_i^{\mathrm{prior}}(1 - K_i) + \gammad \cdot \text{Shock}_i\right)
	\end{equation}

	This placement carries distinct statistical meaning. Trend (injected as process noise on the prior) represents filter-validated evidence of directional drift: it is \emph{already} the output of an EMA that smooths away zero-mean scatter, so it is safe to let it raise $K_i$ and therefore move the mean. Shock (injected as a posterior variance bump) is a provisional response to a single-event surprise: it widens the epistemic uncertainty so that subsequent matches see a larger prior (via Step 2's on-entry aging of the committed posterior, and Step 7's Trend inflation on the next iteration), but it does not force the current mean to chase an unvalidated outlier. If the shock presages a true skill shift, the next match's higher prior will yield a larger $K_i$ and a correspondingly larger mean response; if it was a one-off, the posterior contraction on subsequent normal events returns $V_i$ to its pre-shock level within a few matches without having whipped the mean around.

	\subsubsection*{Step 9: Update Performance Dispersion}
	Behavioral dispersion tracks the zero-mean scatter of a competitor's performance. Define floor and ceiling bounds:
	\begin{equation}
		\sigma_{i,\mathrm{lo}}^2 = 0.001\, V_{\mathrm{start}}, \qquad \sigma_{i,\mathrm{hi}}^2 = V_{\max}
	\end{equation}
	To isolate true erratic execution from underlying skill trends, we calculate the trend-adjusted squared residual by subtracting the momentum $m_i$ from the physical innovation $\ephys$:
	\begin{equation}
		E_i = \max\!\left(0,\; (\ephys - m_i)^2 - V_i^{\mathrm{age}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2\right)
	\end{equation}
	This estimator is approximate: the $Q_i$ scaling attenuates the innovation magnitude rather than exactly removing reliability-penalty components from the squared residual, and the subtracted terms $V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2$ omit the baseline estimation variance $\mathrm{Var}(\bobs)$. In practice, both omissions are conservative---in mature, bulk-finish fields, $Q_i \approx 1$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\bobs)$ is negligible; when novelty, deep-tail, or weak-field penalties dominate, $Q_i$ suppresses the entire driving term. By utilizing the quality-gated physical innovation, untrustworthy observation magnitudes (unanchored topological islands, geometrically unreliable deep tails) do not permanently inflate a competitor's aleatoric profile, while generative physical volatility under a trustworthy log-link does. Furthermore, subtracting $m_i$ prevents the ``aleatoric armor'' pathology: during a rapid skill breakout, a sequence of large positive errors will be absorbed by $m_i$, bringing the trend-adjusted residual near zero and allowing $\sigma_i^2$ to correctly shrink rather than artificially inflate.

	We form an instantaneous target representing the realized behavioral variance for the current observation. To prevent the upper clamp from becoming unrealistically tight when a competitor's current dispersion is very small, we use a reference-anchored hard cap. Let
	\begin{equation}
		\sigma_{i,\mathrm{ref}}^2 = 0.25\,\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2
	\end{equation}
	and define:
	\begin{equation}
		q_i = \mathrm{clamp}\left(E_i,\; \sigma_{i,\mathrm{lo}}^2,\; 9\cdot\,\max\!\left(\sigma_i^2,\sigma_{i,\mathrm{ref}}^2\right)\right)
	\end{equation}
	Re-using the certainty factor $c_i$ defined in Step 6, we construct an effective EMA rate $\beta_i^{\mathrm{eff}}$ to inhibit dispersion growth for unrated or long-inactive competitors:
	\begin{equation}
		\beta_i^{\mathrm{eff}} = \beta \cdot \max\!\left(0.25,\; c_i\right)
	\end{equation}
	Apply the certainty-scaled exponential moving average:
	\begin{equation}
		\sigma_i^2 \leftarrow \mathrm{clamp}\!\left((1 - \beta_i^{\mathrm{eff}})\,\sigma_i^2 + \beta_i^{\mathrm{eff}}\, q_i,\; \sigma_{i,\mathrm{lo}}^2,\; \sigma_{i,\mathrm{hi}}^2\right)
	\end{equation}

	\subsubsection*{Step 10: Commit}
	The posterior $(R_i, V_i, \sigma_i^2, m_i)$ is stored as the committed state. Epistemic aging is not rewritten here: $V_i^{\mathrm{age}}$ is formed on entry to the next event from this committed $V_i$ (Step 2). Skill rust, when enabled, is likewise a dynamic on-entry map of the committed mean (Section C).

	\subsection{Extended Logic for Small and Degenerate Fields}
	Real-world match data routinely violates the assumptions of the pure core loop: fields can be tiny, individual finishes can be extreme outliers, and severe geometric non-linearities exist in the deep tail of ratio scoring\footnote{After an acquaintance whose match performances---specifically winning several recent events by margins $> 0.7$---revealed these additional complexities, the author refers to them collectively as `the Yang pathology'.}. The extended model introduces the parameters from the ``Small and Degenerate Fields'' configuration to robustify the process. These modifications are injected directly into specific steps of the core loop.

	\subsubsection*{A. Log-Score Flooring and Deep-Tail Noise ($\eta(S_i)$)}
	\emph{Modifies Step 1, Step 2, and Step 6.}\\
	Very weak finishes are not reliable linear evidence about top-end skill separation. A competitor finishing at 20\% of the winner is worse than one finishing at 40\%, but the 20\% finisher does not provide proportionally trustworthy information about how far the winner sits above the rest of the field in latent space---nor, dually, about how far that finisher sits below their own latent skill. Deep-tail results are often contaminated by penalties, stage failures, or the nonlinear geometry of far-tail fractions. Critically, $\eta(S_i)$ is \emph{not} competitor-intrinsic generative noise of the form $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2 + \sigma_i^2)$; it is score-dependent observation-model skepticism---heteroskedastic reliability / misspecification variance that grows as $S_i$ enters the deep tail.

	A naive fix would compress the log-score directly, but this destroys the model's clean log-linear predictive semantics. Instead, we keep the expected mean link intact and introduce score-dependent skepticism in the observation variance.

	First, floor the percentages to prevent undefined logarithms:
	\begin{equation}
		L_i = \ln(\max(S_{\mathrm{floor}}, S_i))
	\end{equation}
	Second, introduce a piecewise tail-noise term that grows quadratically below the threshold $s_0$:
	\begin{equation}
		\eta(S_i) =
		\begin{cases}
			0, & S_i \ge s_0 \\
			\xi^2 \left(\frac{s_0 - S_i}{s_0}\right)^2, & S_i < s_0
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	This term plays two dual roles. First, it is added to the denominators of the competitor weights in Step 2 and the pairwise weighting in Step 4, so deep-tail finishes cannot dominate the match anchor:
	\begin{equation}
		W_i \leftarrow \frac{1}{V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2 + \sigma_i^2 + \eta(S_i)}
	\end{equation}
	Second, the same $\eta(S_i)$ enters competitor $i$'s own observation variance in Step 6 (and therefore the $Q_i$ denominator):
	\begin{equation}
		R_{\mathrm{obs},i} \leftarrow R_{\mathrm{obs},i} + \eta(S_i)
	\end{equation}
	The first role protects the field's estimate of $\ln(\xwin)$ from geometrically unreliable anchors. The second is the dual own-update skepticism implied by ``score-dependent skepticism in the observation variance'': a deep-tail finish should not move $R_i$ at full Kalman strength, nor train momentum, dispersion, or Shock as if its residual magnitude were a calibrated generative innovation. Because $\eta(S_i)$ sits outside $R_{\mathrm{clean},i}$, the Observation Quality factor automatically attenuates $\ephys$ and $\erobust$ for deep-tail finishes. Finishes above $s_0$ are unaffected ($\eta = 0$); large mid-pack residuals under a trustworthy log-link remain fully available to Shock.

	\subsubsection*{B. Match-Context Weak-Field Damping ($\sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2$)}
	\label{sec:weakfield}
	\emph{Modifies Step 6.}\\
	Tiny matches in which a large share of the non-winners finish extremely far behind the winner are structurally pathological. The match as a whole provides little trustworthy information about elite top-end skill separation. To model this, we introduce a match-level reliability penalty---the same ontological category as novelty and deep-tail noise.

	Define a small-field taper that decays to zero at $n_{\mathrm{max}}$:

	\begin{equation}
		f_{\mathrm{size}}(N) = \max\!\left(0,\frac{n_{\mathrm{max}} - N}{n_{\mathrm{max}} - 2}\right)
	\end{equation}
	Let $\mathcal{W}$ be the set of non-winners whose scores fall below $s_w$, and define the weak fraction $q = \frac{|\mathcal{W}|}{N - 1}$. If $q < q_0$, no penalty applies. Otherwise, define a depth severity term:
	\begin{equation}
		g = \min\!\left(1,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}| \cdot (-\ln s_w)} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{W}} \max\!\left(0,\ln\!\left(\frac{s_w}{\max(S_{\mathrm{floor}}, S_j)}\right)\right)\right)
	\end{equation}
	The match-level pathology variance is $\sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2 = \omega^2 \cdot (f_{size}(N) \cdot q \cdot g)$. This is added directly into the total observation variance computed in Step 6:
	\begin{equation}
		R_{\mathrm{obs},i} \leftarrow R_{\mathrm{obs},i} + \sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2
	\end{equation}
	Because $\sigma_{\mathrm{path}}^2$ lies outside $R_{\mathrm{clean},i}$, it both throttles the Kalman update $K_i$ for the entire field and reduces $Q_i$, formalizing the heuristic: ``this match may be real, but it is not a precise instrument.''

	\subsubsection*{C. Chronological Mean Reversion (Skill Rust)}
	\emph{Applied on entry with epistemic aging (Step 2), and to all expected rating evaluations.}\\
	In a strict forward-only (online) rating system, the mathematical datum is established by the initial competitor pool. If a pioneer significantly outperforms this naive early field, they are awarded a large positive rating. As the sport matures and historical data accumulates, the filter naturally resolves the true skill of the early demographic downward. However, if that pioneer retires early, their rating is frozen against the obsolete initial datum. When generating modern predictions, this creates a severe artifact where historical retirees appear mathematically superior to active modern champions simply because they avoided the downward shift of the global datum.

	While logistic systems like Glicko suppress this pathology by structurally bounding margins of victory, the Latent Log-Ratio model preserves continuous magnitudes, leaving the artifact exposed. To resolve this without requiring computationally expensive backward smoothing passes over the database, we introduce chronological Mean Reversion (``Skill Rust'').

	Beyond correcting historical datum shifts, mean reversion physically models the perishable nature of the fine motor skills and visual processing required for practical shooting. To avoid artificially punishing competitors for standard off-season breaks, we apply a grace period $t_{\mathrm{grace}}$ (e.g., 0.5 to 1.0 years) before any decay accumulates.

	Let $\Delta t$ be the elapsed time since a competitor's last recorded match, measured in years. The effective time subject to decay is:
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}} = \max(0, \Delta t - t_{\mathrm{grace}})
	\end{equation}

	Using a highly conservative annual decay rate $\lambda_{\mathrm{rust}}$ (e.g., $0.03$, or $3\%$ per year), the aged expected rating is computed dynamically:
	\begin{equation}
		R_i^{\mathrm{age}} = R_{\mathrm{start}} + (R_i - R_{\mathrm{start}}) \cdot \exp(-\lambda_{\mathrm{rust}} \cdot \Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}})
	\end{equation}

	By acting multiplicatively on the log-ratio gap $(R_i - R_{\mathrm{start}})$, the decay is proportionate to the competitor's absolute deviation from the mean. A competitor with an exactly average rating ($R_{\mathrm{start}}$) experiences zero drift, whereas an elite outlier slowly regresses toward the baseline.

	For example, using $t_{\mathrm{grace}} = 0.5$ years and $\lambda_{\mathrm{rust}} = 0.03$: a grandmaster operating at $200\%$ of the baseline field holds a log-rating of $R_i = \ln(2.0) \approx 0.693$. If they take a 1.5-year hiatus, their effective decay time is 1.0 year. Their aged rating becomes $R_i^{\mathrm{age}} = 0.693 \cdot \exp(-0.03) \approx 0.672$, which translates to an expected absolute performance of $\exp(0.672) \approx 1.958\times$ the baseline.

	In the core algorithm, $R_i^{\mathrm{age}}$ strictly replaces $R_i$ during the calculation of baseline residuals ($a_i$ in Step 3), pairwise expected margins (Step 4), observation innovation ($e_i$ in Step 6), the Kalman mean update (Step 7), and all out-of-sample predictive mixtures (Section~\ref{sec:prediction}).


	\subsection{Handling Non-Ratio Anomalies: Time Bonuses and Micro-Stages}
	The Latent Log-Ratio model fundamentally assumes that the scoring data exists on a \textbf{Ratio Scale} (where zero represents an absolute absence of time, and ratios between values are physically meaningful). However, specific rule sets can violate this assumption:

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Time Bonuses (ICORE):} Rules that subtract fixed time for accuracy can result in near-zero or even negative stage times. This converts the data into an Interval Scale, where log-ratios are mathematically undefined or heavily distorted.
		\item \textbf{Micro-Stages (Speed Shoots):} Even without bonuses, very fast stages (e.g., a 2.0-second stage) cause artificial dispersion magnification. A 0.5-second stumble on a 2.0-second stage yields a massive 20\% ratio penalty ($T_{min}/T = 2.0 / 2.5 = 0.8$), whereas a 0.5-second stumble on a 20-second stage is mathematically negligible.
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsubsection*{Additive Time Normalization (Time Padding)}
	To restore the ratio-scale properties and prevent infinite variance from undefined logarithms, we apply an additive normalization constant ($k$) to all raw times on stages that fall below a minimum time threshold ($T_{\mathrm{floor}}$).

	Let $T_{min}$ be the winning (lowest) time on a given stage. If $T_{min} < T_{\mathrm{floor}}$ (e.g., $T_{\mathrm{floor}} = 10.0$ seconds), we calculate a padding constant:
	\begin{equation}
		k = T_{\mathrm{floor}} - T_{min}
	\end{equation}

	This constant is then added to every competitor's time on that stage before converting the finishes into percentages:
	\begin{equation}
		T_i' = T_i + k
	\end{equation}

	This translation guarantees that all $T_i' \geq T_{\mathrm{floor}} > 0$, ensuring the natural logarithm is always defined. Furthermore, it acts as mathematical smoothing (similar to Laplace smoothing). It preserves the exact rank-order of the competitors while compressing the artificially inflated log-ratios back to a scale representative of true human skill differences.

	We only scale up from below to solve mathematical issues: $T_{\mathrm{floor}}$ corrects undefined-log problems and smooths extreme dispersion swings on short stages. Scaling long stages down to a reference time introduces issues rather than correcting them:

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{The ``Speed Tax'' on Field Courses (Reversed Dispersion):}
		While global scaling fixes the impact upon dispersion of fixed-time mistakes, it introduces massive mathematical volatility for proportional speed differences. If a competitor's physiological pace is exactly 10\% slower than the stage winner, their absolute time difference ($\Delta T$) scales with the length of the stage.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item On a short 10-second stage: $\Delta T = 1.0$ second. Under a universal reference (e.g., $T_{ref} = 20.0$), their ratio is $\frac{21.0}{20.0} = 1.05$ (a 5\% penalty).
			\item On a long 50-second stage: $\Delta T = 5.0$ seconds. Under the same universal reference, their ratio is $\frac{25.0}{20.0} = 1.25$ (a 25\% penalty).
		\end{itemize}
		By standardizing the winner's time universally, the model artificially magnifies the deficit of consistently slower competitors on long field courses.

		\item \textbf{Loss of Physical Fidelity:}
		In practical shooting, the duration of the stage provides critical context for the severity of a mistake. A 3.0-second error on a 5.0-second stage represents a catastrophic failure relative to the task, whereas a 3.0-second error on a 50.0-second stage is a minor inefficiency. A universal additive scale discards the physical reality of the task's duration, treating both errors as mathematically identical.
	\end{enumerate}

	Because of these drawbacks, a \textbf{Hybrid Scaling} approach is generally preferred over a universal reference time. By applying the additive padding $k$ only to stages where $T_{min} < T_{\mathrm{floor}}$, the model safely bounds the extreme punishment of micro-stages and time bonuses, while leaving standard field courses unadjusted to preserve the mathematical reality of proportional pace.

	\section{The Prediction System and Confidence Intervals}
	\label{sec:prediction}

	Because ratings operate in log space, predicted finish ratios use log-normal bookkeeping. The system constructs a mixture distribution over plausible winners to calculate expected finish percentages and predictive confidence intervals.

	\subsection{Expected Finish Ratio}
	Let $p_w$ be the mixture weights established by the candidate probabilities. Calculating the expected finish ratio $S_i$ using a closed-form approximation requires resolving two distinct statistical boundaries: the Extreme Value behavior of the winner, and the right-truncation of the percentage scale.

	\subsubsection*{The Max Order Correction ($\Delta_w$)}
	In wide-open fields with many realistic contenders, the actual match winner will not shoot their average expected performance; they will be the maximum order statistic of the contender pool. To correct for this without requiring a full Monte Carlo simulation, we calculate the effective number of contenders using the Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR), which naturally filters out the long tail of unlikely winners:
	\begin{equation}
		N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \left(\sum_w p_w^2\right)^{-1}
	\end{equation}

	We find the expected maximum of $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ variables using Blom's approximation. However, a naive application of Extreme Value Theory assumes that performance variance is perfectly symmetric and unbounded. In practical shooting, a competitor's performance is dominated by downside risk (mistakes, penalties, equipment malfunctions), while their maximum potential speed is biomechanically bounded by human visual processing and mechanical cycle rates.

	To preserve Extreme Value Theory assumptions regarding cohort covariance, we must strictly isolate the independent fraction of the variance. We partition the epistemic uncertainty using $(1 - \rho)$, and rely on the purely idiosyncratic prediction sport noise $\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2$ to represent match-day variance. The presumed winner's rating is shifted upward by this corrected, dampened extreme-value expectation:
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta_w = \Phi^{-1}\Big(\frac{N_\mathrm{eff} - 0.375}{N_\mathrm{eff} + 0.25}\Big) \cdot \sqrt{V_w^{\mathrm{age}}(1 - \rho) + (\kappa \cdot \tilde{\sigma}_w^2) + \sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2}
	\end{equation}
	The adjusted pairwise log-difference between competitor $i$ and presumed winner $w$ is therefore $\tilde{\mu}_{iw} = R_i - (R_w + \Delta_w)$. For matches with a single dominant favorite ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 1$), the Blom approximation naturally evaluates to zero, preserving unbiased predictions.

	\subsubsection*{The Truncation Correction ($\Lambda_{iw}$)}
	Because $S_i$ represents a score relative to the match winner, it is strictly bounded by $S_i \le 1.0$. Conditioning the prediction on $w$ being the winner implicitly conditions the expected ratio on $L_i \le L_w$. We enforce this physical ceiling by defining a right-truncation modifier (truncated at $0$ in log space) for the log-normal expectation:
	\begin{equation}
		\Lambda_{iw} = \frac{\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{iw} + \sigma_{iw}^2}{\sigma_{iw}}\right)}{\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{iw}}{\sigma_{iw}}\right)}
	\end{equation}
	where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. Because competitors share structural cohort uncertainty (formalized by the intraclass correlation $\rho$), the epistemic variance of their pairwise difference must subtract their covariance. The adjusted pairwise variance is:

	\begin{equation}
		\sigma_{iw}^2 = V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + V_w^{\mathrm{age}} - 2\rho\sqrt{V_i^{\mathrm{age}} V_w^{\mathrm{age}}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2 + \kappa(\tilde{\sigma}_i^2 + \tilde{\sigma}_w^2)
	\end{equation}

	The denominator isolates the probability mass to the universe where $w$ beats $i$, and the numerator perfectly cancels the mass of the unconstrained integral that lies above 1.0.

	\subsubsection*{The Final Expected Ratio}
	By the law of total expectation over the random winner, applying both the max order shift and the truncation boundary, the expected ratio of competitor $i$ to the winner is:
	\begin{equation}
		\hat{S}_i = (p_i \cdot 1.0) + \sum_{w \neq i} p_w \left[ \exp\!\left(\tilde{\mu}_{iw} + \frac{\sigma_{iw}^2}{2}\right) \cdot \Lambda_{iw} \right]
	\end{equation}
	The isolated $p_i$ term handles the mixture branch where $i$ is the presumed winner, yielding a ratio of exactly 1.0. For all other candidate winners, this formulation safely resolves the winner's curse bias in large fields while strictly obeying the $100\%$ scaling maximum.

	\subsection{Predictive Variance and Confidence Intervals}
	To generate predictive confidence intervals, we calculate the total log-space predictive variance using the Law of Total Variance over the mixture distribution of possible winners. The total variance is the sum of the average predictive uncertainty assuming the winner is known (Within-Component) and the variance introduced by the uncertainty of who wins (Between-Component).

	The Within-Component variance evaluates the expected pairwise uncertainty against the presumed winner. Because the predictive baseline is formed by the difference of two ratings ($R_i - R_w$), we must calculate the variance of that difference. This requires subtracting the shared cohort covariance ($\rho \sqrt{V_i V_w}$) from their combined epistemic variances.

	The sport contribution $2\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2$ represents the idiosyncratic match-day noise for both competitors, and $\kappa \in [0,1]$ scales the projection of their certainty-discounted behavioral dispersions ($\tilde{\sigma}^2$). The total probability-weighted Within-Component variance is:
	\begin{equation}
		\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{within}}^2 = \sum_{w \neq i} p_w \left[ V_i^{\mathrm{age}} + V_w^{\mathrm{age}} - 2\rho\sqrt{V_i^{\mathrm{age}} V_w^{\mathrm{age}}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2 + \kappa(\tilde{\sigma}_i^2 + \tilde{\sigma}_w^2) \right]
	\end{equation}

	Because the focal competitor's expected score shifts depending on which universe occurs, we must calculate the Between-Component variance. We first establish the unconditional expected log-score (Grand Mean) across all universes:
	\begin{equation}
		\bar{L}_i = \sum_{w \neq i} p_w \tilde{\mu}_{iw}
	\end{equation}

	The Between-Component variance is the probability-weighted variance of the conditional means around this Grand Mean. In the specific universe where competitor $i$ wins, their conditional log-score is exactly $0$. The sum is therefore:
	\begin{equation}
		\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{between}}^2 = p_i (\bar{L}_i^2) + \sum_{w \neq i} p_w (\tilde{\mu}_{iw} - \bar{L}_i)^2
	\end{equation}

	The total predictive variance in log space is the sum of both components:
	\begin{equation}
		\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2 = \varsigma_{i,\mathrm{within}}^2 + \varsigma_{i,\mathrm{between}}^2
	\end{equation}

	\subsubsection*{Moment-Matched Log-Normal Approximation}
	The predictive distribution is formally a mixture of right-truncated log-normals (one component per candidate winner $w \neq i$), plus a degenerate component at $1.0$ for the branch in which competitor $i$ is the winner. This mixture has no closed-form median or quantiles and therefore does not directly yield a multiplicative $\pm 1\sigma$ interval of known probability coverage.

	To produce a principled closed-form band, we approximate the mixture by the single log-normal $\mathrm{LN}(\mu_i^\star,\ \varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2)$ whose moments match the mixture exactly in two respects: its arithmetic mean on the ratio scale equals $\hat{S}_i$, and its variance on the log scale equals $\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2$. Both quantities are derived above by the laws of total expectation and total variance, so no further assumptions about the mixture structure are needed. Solving the log-normal expectation identity $\hat{S}_i = \exp(\mu_i^\star + \varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2/2)$ for $\exp(\mu_i^\star)$ yields the median of the approximating distribution in ratio space:
	\begin{equation}
		\tilde{m}_i = \hat{S}_i \cdot \exp\!\left(-\frac{\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2}{2}\right)
	\end{equation}
	The approximation is exact when the mixture collapses to a single component (e.g., when one $p_w \to 1$ and $\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{between}}^2 \to 0$) and remains tight when one favorite dominates the winner distribution. It is least faithful for wide-open fields with several equally likely winners, where the true mixture is visibly multimodal and no single log-normal can capture that shape; in such cases the approximation should be understood as an interpretable summary rather than a literal distributional claim.

	\subsubsection*{Confidence Intervals}
	Because the rating system operates natively in log space, confidence intervals in ratio space are fundamentally multiplicative. We define the geometric standard deviation of the approximating log-normal as:
	\begin{equation}
		\mathrm{GSD}_i = \exp\!\left(\sqrt{\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2}\right)
	\end{equation}
	The $\pm 1\sigma$ predictive bounds are applied multiplicatively around the approximation median $\tilde{m}_i$:
	\begin{equation}
		S_{i,\mathrm{lower}} = \frac{\tilde{m}_i}{\mathrm{GSD}_i}, \qquad S_{i,\mathrm{upper}} = \tilde{m}_i \times \mathrm{GSD}_i
	\end{equation}
	Centering the band on $\tilde{m}_i$ rather than $\hat{S}_i$ is the standard treatment for log-normal intervals: a log-normal's $\pm 1\sigma$ probability mass is symmetric in log space around its \emph{median}, not its arithmetic mean, which sits above the median by a factor of $\exp(\varsigma_{i,\mathrm{total}}^2/2)$. Under the moment-matched approximation, the band $[\tilde{m}_i/\mathrm{GSD}_i,\ \tilde{m}_i \cdot \mathrm{GSD}_i]$ therefore carries the usual $68.27\%$ coverage interpretation; for the true mixture, the coverage is close to this value and deviates only as the mixture departs from a single log-normal.

	Notably, while both $\hat{S}_i$ and $\tilde{m}_i$ are strictly bounded to $1.0$, the upper confidence interval $S_{i,\mathrm{upper}}$ is permitted to exceed $1.0$. This is physically meaningful: an upper bound of $1.02$ indicates that a competitor's $+1\sigma$ performance would theoretically exceed the presumed winner's expected performance by 2\%, visually indicating to users that the competitor has a realistic probability of winning the match.

	\section{Comparison with Earlier Work}
	In the course of producing analysis on the shooting sports over several years, the author has read about and experimented with a number of rating algorithms.

	\subsection{Glicko-2}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Native Representation of Performance Distances:} Logistic models represent the outcome of a pairwise comparison as a probability in $(0, 1)$, which is correct for binary win/loss but introduces information loss for continuous margins. A pairwise expected score of $0.95$ could correspond to a 5\% performance margin or a 50\% margin---the sigmoid saturates at the extremes, compressing large differences into a narrow band. The latent log-ratio model preserves the log-ratio structure of performance distances globally: a competitor who performs at twice the level of another has a log-gap of $\ln(2) \approx 0.69$, regardless of whether both are elite or both are novices. Pathological deep-tail updates are handled by observation-noise inflation rather than by deforming this distance scale, so prediction remains analytically simple.

		\item \textbf{Explicit Separation of Variance Sources:} Rather than routing all uncertainty through a single mechanism (e.g., Glicko-2's Rating Deviation, which conflates measurement uncertainty, skill drift, and competitor consistency), the model explicitly separates:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item $\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2$: the irreducible noise of the sport for \emph{updates} (a global constant, tuned once),
			\item $\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2$: idiosyncratic sport noise used only in \emph{predictions} (win probabilities and predictive bands; typically smaller than $\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2$),
			\item $V_i$: measurement uncertainty in each competitor's rating (adapts via the Kalman filter),
			\item $\sigma_i^2$: per-competitor performance dispersion for \emph{updates} (certainty-scaled EMA toward a clamped target); optionally scaled by $\kappa$ for \emph{predictive} bands only,
			\item $\sigma_{drift}^2$: skill drift over time (a global constant for the aging function).
		\end{itemize}
		This decomposition makes each parameter independently interpretable and tunable. In Glicko-2, a competitor bombing a single stage can drastically inflate their RD (via the volatility $\rightarrow$ RD pathway), destabilizing future updates. Here, a single bad performance primarily affects $\sigma_i^2$ (which adapts gradually via the certainty-scaled EMA and a capped driving term) and may trigger a modest $V_i$ increase (via the innovation term), but neither mechanism produces the runaway instability that Glicko-2's volatility iteration can exhibit.
	\end{enumerate}

	\section{Parameter List}
	\label{app:parameters}

		The parameters to the Latent Log-Ratio system fall into five categories: scaling, to convert the internal log units to user-friendly display numbers; core parameters, which drive the main algorithm; parameters that improve performance on small or degenerate fields; parameters that improve observed performance when converting from ratings to predicted ratios; and chronological mean-reversion parameters for inactivity handling.

	\subsubsection*{Scaling (Display $s$, $b$)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$s$, $b$ (Display Scale):} User-facing ratings use an affine map $R^{\mathrm{disp}} = s\cdot R + b$. Variance and dispersion in display space scale as $s^2$ times latent variance; display standard deviations scale as $|s|$ times latent standard deviation.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection*{Core Parameters -- General}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2$ (Sport Variance):} A tuned constant representing the inherent noise of a single match observation in the sport. This is the irreducible variance that even a perfectly consistent competitor experiences due to environmental factors, range conditions, and the stochastic nature of shooting. Tuned once per sport; does not vary per competitor.
		\item \textbf{$V_{\mathrm{start}}$ (Starting Variance):} The variance assigned to competitors with no rating history, i.e. prior width for new shooters.
		\item \textbf{$V_{\max}$ (Maximum Variance):} Upper bound on committed rating variance after Kalman updates, surprise adaptation, and after drift aging.
		\item \textbf{$\sigma_{\mathrm{drift}}^2$ (Skill Drift Rate):} The rate at which rating uncertainty grows during periods of inactivity. Represents the possibility that a competitor's true skill changes when they are not being observed. Applied on entry to each event as $V_i^{\mathrm{age}} = \min(V_{\max},\, V_i + \sigma_{\mathrm{drift}}^2 \times \Delta t)$, where $V_i$ is the last committed posterior and $\Delta t$ is measured in rating periods.
		\item \textbf{$\beta$ (Dispersion Adaptation Rate):} Base rate for the performance dispersion EMA, multiplied by a certainty factor derived from $V_i^{\mathrm{age}}/V_{\max}$ (bounded below by $0.25\,\beta$).
		\item \textbf{$\gamma$ (Surprise Adaptation Rate):} Controls how aggressively the variance $V_i$ responds to unexpectedly surprising results. Recommended range: 0.05--0.1.
		\item \textbf{$\lambda$ (Momentum Adaptation Rate):} The base rate for the innovation momentum tracker.
		\item \textbf{$\alpha$ (Pairwise Blend Weight):} Controls how much structural information from pairwise opponent comparisons is blended into the baseline-derived observed performance. When $\alpha = 0$, the model reduces to the pure baseline model.
		\item \textbf{$\nudof$ (Student-$t$ Degrees of Freedom):} Heaviness of the mean-update M-estimator \(w_t(z)\). Smaller \(\nudof\) damps moderate outliers more aggressively; \(\nudof \to \infty\) recovers an undamped Gaussian mean update. Default: \(4\).
		\item \textbf{$\ctcut$ (Student-$t$ Cutoff):} Full-strength innovation band, in residual sigmas, before \(w_t\) begins to taper. May be split by the sign of the innovation. Setting \(\ctcut = 1\) recovers the standard Student-$t$ kernel.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection*{Core Parameters -- Off-Diagonal Covariance Approximation}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$\rho$ (Cohort Intraclass Correlation):} A global correlation coefficient representing the fraction of epistemic uncertainty assumed to be shared structurally among competitors (e.g., a local club whose ratings are accurate relative to each other, but floating relative to the global mean). Because this covariance mathematically scales with the absolute epistemic variance of the field, it exerts massive influence on new cohorts but gracefully vanishes for established veterans. In practice, $\rho$ prevents certainty collapse (the ``Day 1'' rating explosion) by explicitly bounding the baseline variance to a cohort asymptote, preventing the filter from falsely trusting an uncertain field's average as a rigid mathematical datum.
		\item \textbf{$k_{\mathrm{max}}$ (Graph Maturity Threshold):} The number of historical events at which a competitor is assumed to be fully integrated into the global network. Field novelty uses a $W$-weighted mean of the per-competitor maturity fractions $\mu_i$, so $\bar{\mu}$ tracks the history of the precision mass that sets the typical match anchor.
		\item \textbf{$\psi^2$ (Novelty Variance):} The maximum noise penalty applied to topologically isolated, immature fields. Along with $k_{\mathrm{max}}$, it limits the impact of extreme performance margins shot in low-history networks: ``The margins between these competitors are directionally meaningful, but their absolute magnitudes are untrustworthy as a measure of global top-end skill.'' Fundamentally, $k_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $\psi^2$ prevent the filter from falsely trusting the field's structural variance.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection*{Small and Degenerate Fields}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$c$ (Baseline Robustness Threshold):} A Huber-style threshold, measured in residual standard deviations, used to downweight extreme field anchors when estimating match baseline.
		\item \textbf{$s_0$ (Tail Noise Start Percentage):} The finish percentage below which very weak results begin receiving additional observation noise. Above $s_0$, the ordinary variance model is used.
		\item \textbf{$\xi^2$ (Tail Noise Variance):} The maximum additional observation variance applied to the deepest tail. Larger values make the model more skeptical of very weak finishes without changing the log-ratio mean link.
		\item \textbf{$\omega^2$ (Weak-Field Variance):} The maximum additional match-context observation variance applied when a field is both small and bottom-heavy.
		\item \textbf{$n_{\mathrm{max}}$ (Weak-Field Max Size):} The field size at or above which weak-field damping decays to zero.
		\item \textbf{$s_w$ (Weak Finish Threshold):} The finish threshold below which a non-winning score counts as ``weak'' for weak-field detection.
		\item \textbf{$q_0$ (Weak Fraction Threshold):} The minimum fraction of weak non-winners required before the weak-field term activates.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection*{Prediction Parameters}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2$ (Prediction Sport Variance):} Idiosyncratic sport noise used only in the prediction pipeline (win-probability denominators and predictive variance).
		\item \textbf{$\kappa$ (Prediction Behavioral Scale):} Dimensionless coefficient in $[0,1]$ that scales how much per-competitor behavioral variance $\sigma_i^2$ enters \emph{posterior predictive} variance for finish-ratio bands.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsubsection*{Mean Reversion Parameters}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{$t_{\mathrm{grace}}$ (Rust Grace Period):} Inactivity duration, measured in years, before chronological mean-reversion begins. For elapsed time $\Delta t$ since last match, decay uses $\Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}} = \max(0, \Delta t - t_{\mathrm{grace}})$.
		\item \textbf{$\lambda_{\mathrm{rust}}$ (Rust Decay Rate):} Exponential decay coefficient, measured in inverse years, controlling how quickly inactive competitors regress toward baseline after grace time. Applied through $\exp(-\lambda_{\mathrm{rust}} \cdot \Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}})$ as described in subsection E.
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Practical Initialization and Tuning Guide}
	\label{app:tuning}

	Because the Latent Log-Ratio model decomposes uncertainty into strictly separated physical mechanisms, attempting to tune the model intuitively using human-readable standard deviations often leads to severe pathologies (e.g., the ``Square Root Deception,'' where users misjudge the gravitational pull of precision-weighted means).

	Tuning must follow a structured, normalized sequence. The irreducible match noise of the sport acts as the fundamental physical anchor ($1.0\,\mathrm{SV}$). All epistemic and aleatoric variances are set as multiples of this unit, followed by adjusting the dimensionless adaptation rates.

	\subsection{Phase 1: Determine the Anchor ($1.0\,\mathrm{SV}$)}
	The Sport Variance ($\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2$) represents the inherent, irreducible background noise of a single match observation in the sport (environmental factors, lighting, stage design).
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Identify a cohort of highly consistent, active veterans.
		\item Measure the steady-state variance of their log-score residuals.
		\item Base $\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2 \equiv 1.0\,\mathrm{SV}$ on this empirical variance. If a consistent pro fluctuates by $\pm 3\%$ match-to-match, $\sqrt{1.0\,\mathrm{SV}} \approx 0.03$.
	\end{enumerate}

	Because this empirical measurement inherently contains its own estimation error, and indeed is challenging to measure in sports where environmental factors like weather or course difficulty disguise the underlying signal, small adjustments to $\sigma_{\mathrm{sport}}^2$ may be practically useful. By tweaking \emph{just} the Sport Variance (while holding the epistemic parameters like $V_{\mathrm{start}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{drift}}^2$ at their established absolute values), it acts as a thermodynamic ``temperature'' dial for the model:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Raising Sport Variance (Cooling):} The model assumes the environment is noisier than strictly measured. Total expected uncertainty ($T_i$) grows relative to the immutable physical match residuals ($e_i$). This drops the Kalman gain slightly and makes the non-linear Variance Surprise ($e_i^2 > T_i$) harder to trigger, resulting in a smoother, less reactive filter. Moving too far in this direction will yield a filter that fails to update on true changes to the underlying competitor variables.
		\item \textbf{Lowering Sport Variance (Heating):} The model assumes a more sterile environment than measured. $T_i$ shrinks, making the filter more reactive. Moving too far in this direction will yield highly volatile ratings, over-updating on normal fluctuations.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Phase 2: Establish the Golden Ratios (Units of $\mathrm{SV}$)}
	With the baseline $1.0\,\mathrm{SV}$ defined, initialize the system's variance-space parameters as relative multiples of this anchor.

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Skill Drift Rate ($\sigma_{\mathrm{drift}}^2$): $0.5\,\mathrm{SV}$ per year (Range: $0.1 - 1.0$).}
		Adult motor skills degrade slowly. Half a match's worth of uncertainty per year allows veterans to remain stable while preventing absolute certainty collapse.
		\item \textbf{Starting Variance ($V_{\mathrm{start}}$): $25.0\,\mathrm{SV}$ (Range: $20.0 - 40.0$).}
		New competitors require enough variance to traverse the demographic distribution rapidly. If competitor graphs often show an initial spike followed by decay, tune down slightly.
		\item \textbf{Maximum Variance ($V_{\max}$): $33.0\,\mathrm{SV}$ (Range: $30.0 - 50.0$).}
		An absolute computational ceiling slightly higher than $V_{\mathrm{start}}$, allowing returning shooters or massive variance surprises to snap the Kalman gain back to near 1.0 without overflowing the arithmetic. Note that the certainty parameter $c_i$ defined in \ref{step6}, Step 6, scales the update speed of dispersion $\sigma_i^2$ and momentum $m_i$, so $V_{\max}$ should be moved in proportion with $V_\mathrm{start}$ to prevent unintentional changes to the movement of other parameters.
		\item \textbf{Initial Dispersion ($\sigma_{i,\mathrm{init}}^2$): $0.1\,\mathrm{SV}$ (Range: $0.05 - 0.25$).}
		Competitors must be treated as consistent until proven otherwise. Initializing dispersion at a fraction of $\mathrm{SV}$ ensures the Kalman denominator remains small, allowing unhindered early rating convergence.
		\item \textbf{Tail Noise ($\xi^2$): $\approx 33.0\,\mathrm{SV}$:}
		Requires empirical tuning based on the sport's deep-tail geometry. Set high enough to collapse the update weight of catastrophic failures to near-zero, if needed.
		\item \textbf{Weak-Field Variance ($\omega^2$): $\approx 200.0\,\mathrm{SV}$.}
		A massive structural penalty applied to degenerate, small-and-weak fields, ensuring they provide virtually no mathematically rigid evidence about elite top-end skill separation.
		\item \textbf{Novelty Variance ($\psi^2$): $5.0 \mathrm{SV}$ (Range: $1.0 - 25.0$).}
		Novelty variance serves as a light regularizing factor on fields of predominantly new competitors. It targets high-level competitors who appear and retire early in a dataset, earning incorrectly high ratings against a competitor graph that is not yet widely connected. $5.0$ represents minimal damping, but is experimentally sufficient to correct most unexpected outliers without unduly slowing the number of events required for new groups of ratings to converge.
		\item \textbf{Prediction Sport Variance ($\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2$): $0.0\,\mathrm{SV}$.}
		Philosophically, predictive uncertainty should scale with the competitors' actual historical behaviors. Set this to zero and use $\kappa$ (see Phase 3) to widen predictive error bars. Only inject flat $\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2$ if $\kappa$ is fully saturated at $1.0$ and predictions remain too narrow, or if the underlying conditions of the sport produce idiosyncratic variance that cannot be attributed to differences in competitor skill.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection{Phase 3: Dimensionless Parameters and Thresholds}
	Finally, tune the adaptation rates, blend weights, and Huber thresholds.

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Prediction Behavioral Scale ($\kappa$):} \textbf{(Range: $0.0 - 1.0$).} Use as the primary tool for predictive error bar size control. It scales how much of a competitor's behavioral dispersion ($\sigma_i^2$) is projected into their published predictive bands.
		\item \textbf{Cohort Intraclass Correlation ($\rho$): $0.3$ (Range: $\ge 0$).}
		Prefer the lowest value that reliably eliminates the ``Day 1 Rating Explosion'' (the artifact where unrated fields collapse baseline variance to zero). The observed symptom of this problem is early competitors in weak or unknown fields climbing to the top of the ratings, while empirically higher-skilled competitors struggle to reach the same region after the cold start has completed. Increasing this parameter will damp early changes in any new group of ratings by increasing observational noise and thereby decreasing the Kalman gain.
		\item \textbf{Momentum Adaptation Rate ($\lambda$): $0.2$}
		Controls the EMA for tracking directional skill trends. The half-life of an exponential moving average is given by $ln(0.5) / ln(1 - \alpha)$.
		The default yields a half-life of $\approx 3.1$ events. A relatively short half-life (according to the schedule of the sport) yields faster reaction to both the beginning and end of a streak.
		\item \textbf{Dispersion Adaptation Rate ($\beta$): $0.1$.}
		Controls the EMA for behavioral volatility.The default value of $0.1$ yields a half-life of $\approx 6.6$ events. A long window relative to the sport's schedule (and to $\lambda$) prevents too-eager increases in dispersion, which can lead to the ``aleatoric armor'' pathology.
		\item \textbf{Surprise Adaptation Rate ($\gamma$): $0.1$.}
		Controls how aggressively $V_i$ inflates when the momentum or squared error breaches expected variance. Depends on event frequency and the nature of skill shocks in the sport.
		\item \textbf{Pairwise Blend Weight ($\alpha$): $0.1$ (Range: $0.0 - 1.0$).}
		Empirically, precision-weighted global baselines are more reliable than sparse pairwise links. However, blending a small amount of pairwise data ($\alpha=0.1$) allows the system to catch localized environmental signals (e.g., competitors scheduled for different days with different weather) or asymmetries in field composition (e.g., a weak center but a strong top end) that the global baseline smooths over.
		\item \textbf{Student-$t$ Degrees of Freedom ($\nudof$): $4$ (Range: $2$--$10$).}
		Controls how fast \(w_t\) falls once \(|z|\) exceeds \(\ctcut\). \(\nudof = 4\) is the usual robust-$t$ default (\(\approx 29\%\) damping at \(z = -1.75\) when \(\ctcut = 1\)). Raising \(\nudof\) toward \(8\)--$10$ softens the taper.
		\item \textbf{Student-$t$ Cutoff ($\ctcut$): $2$ (Range: $0$--$3$; optionally signed).}
		Where the taper \emph{starts}. \(1.0\) begins immediately past \(1\sigma\); \(2.0\) lets typical matches through; a smaller downside cutoff (e.g.\ \(1.0\)) with a larger upside cutoff is the one-sided extension when contamination is known to be negative. \(\ctcut\) and \(\nudof\) interact: a tight cutoff with large \(\nudof\) still barely damps.
		\item \textbf{Baseline Robustness ($c$): $2.5$.}
		A Huber-style threshold marking the boundary between $L_2$ (squared) and $L_1$ (absolute) penalty weighting. A value of 2.5 means the filter trusts residuals normally up to 2.5 standard deviations, but linearly downweights the influence of outliers beyond that. Lower values (e.g., 2.0) aggressively clip outliers in highly volatile sports; higher values (3.0+) trust the field's arithmetic mean more strictly.
		\item \textbf{Tail Noise Start ($s_0$): $0.6$.}
		The finish percentage below which the $\xi^2$ tail noise begins applying. Tune depending on the severity of the winner's curse and the geometry of the sport's deep tail.
		\item \textbf{Weak-Field Thresholds ($n_{\mathrm{max}}, s_w, q_0$):}
		Highly dependent on sport demographics. Define the size and shape of a match that should be legally penalized by $\omega^2$.
		\item \textbf{Graph Maturity Threshold ($k_\mathrm{max}$): $8.0$.}
		How quickly novelty variance $\psi^2$ decays as the precision mass of the field accumulates history. Dependent on sport demographics. For values between 5 and 20, the penalty reaches half strength at approximately $0.2 \cdot k_\mathrm{max}$.
	\end{itemize}
